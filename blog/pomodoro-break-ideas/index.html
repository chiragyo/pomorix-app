What to Do on a Pomodoro Break: 25 Ideas That Actually Recharge You

The best things to do in a 5-minute or 30-minute Pomodoro break, what to avoid, and how to make sure your break ends on time.

Key takeaways

  • A good break changes what you are doing: move your body, rest your eyes, and give your mind a rest from new input.
  • Avoid social media, news and email. They don't rest your attention and they rarely end on time.
  • Short breaks (5 minutes) are for movement and water. Long breaks (15 to 30 minutes) are for food, walks and real rest.
  • Let a timer end the break for you, so getting back to work isn't a decision.

The break is the most underrated part of the Pomodoro Technique. Do it well and you come back sharper. Do it badly, scrolling your phone for “just a minute”, and the break swallows the next session too.

Here are 25 break ideas that actually recharge you, sorted by how much time you have, plus the habits that keep breaks from running over.

What makes a good break?

A good break is a change, not more of the same. If you've been sitting and staring at a screen while thinking hard, the best break involves moving, looking at something far away and letting your mind rest from new input.

Research backs this up. A 2022 meta-analysis of micro-break studies found that even short breaks boost energy and reduce fatigue. Another line of research found that people who briefly switched their attention away from a long task held their performance better than those who pushed on without a pause. The break isn't time stolen from work; it's what keeps the work good.

Three questions to judge a break activity:

  1. Does it get me off the chair or away from the screen?
  2. Does it rest my attention rather than fill it with new stuff?
  3. Will it end on time on its own?

5-minute break ideas

Move

  1. Stand up and stretch. Neck, shoulders, back and wrists, especially after typing.
  2. Walk to get water. Hydration and movement in one.
  3. Climb a flight of stairs or two. A quick lift in alertness.
  4. Do 10 squats or push-ups. Gets blood moving without making you sweaty.
  5. Shake out your body or put on one song and dance. It sounds silly; it works.

Rest your eyes and mind

  1. Look out of a window at something far away. Your eyes have been focused at arm's length for 25 minutes.
  2. Close your eyes and breathe slowly for a minute. Try breathing in for 4 counts and out for 6.
  3. Step outside for daylight and fresh air, even for two minutes.
  4. Do nothing. Sit and let your mind wander. Mind-wandering is a feature, not a bug: it's when ideas connect.

Small resets

  1. Refill your water or make tea.
  2. Tidy your desk for a minute. A clear desk makes the next session easier to start.
  3. Write down what you'll do next, so the next session starts instantly.
  4. Eat a small snack like fruit or nuts if you're hungry.
  5. Play a short, self-contained game that ends on its own, not an endless feed.

15 to 30-minute break ideas (long breaks)

  1. Go for a walk, ideally outside and without your phone in hand.
  2. Eat a proper meal away from your desk.
  3. Do a short workout or yoga session.
  4. Take a power nap of 10 to 20 minutes if you're flagging in the afternoon. Set an alarm.
  5. Call a friend or family member for a real conversation.
  6. Do a household chore like dishes or laundry. Simple physical tasks are surprisingly restful.
  7. Cook or prep food for later.
  8. Play an instrument or sketch.
  9. Read something unrelated for fun: fiction, on paper ideally.
  10. Water the plants, step into the garden or sit somewhere green.
  11. Meditate for 10 minutes.

What to avoid on breaks

  • Social media and short videos. Built never to end. Five minutes becomes twenty, and you return with a head full of other people's stuff.
  • Email and work chat. You'll find something that needs a reply, and your break becomes work.
  • News. Often stressful, never finished.
  • Starting another task. “I'll just quickly…” is how you lose the benefit of the break.

If your phone is the problem, you're not alone. Read how to stop checking your phone while you study or work.

How to make breaks end on time

The hardest part of a break isn't starting it; it's ending it. A few tricks help:

  • Let a timer end the break. When it rings, the decision is made for you.
  • Leave a breadcrumb. Before the break, write the next step. Coming back is easier when you know exactly what to do.
  • Auto-start the next session if you tend to drift. Many apps can start focus automatically after a break.
  • Stay close. On short breaks, don't go anywhere you can't return from in a minute.

Questions people ask

What should I not do on a Pomodoro break?

Avoid anything that pulls you into new information or is hard to stop: social media, news, email, chat, videos and games with no natural end. Also avoid starting another work task, which defeats the point of the break.

Is it OK to use my phone on a study break?

Briefly replying to a message is fine, but scrolling feeds is the most common way breaks turn from 5 minutes into 25. If you do use your phone, decide what for before you pick it up, and let a timer end the break.

How long should a Pomodoro break be?

Classically 5 minutes after each 25-minute session, and 15 to 30 minutes after every four sessions. If you use longer focus sessions, lengthen the break to about one fifth of the focus time.

Should I take a break even if I'm in the flow?

Usually yes. A quick break while you still know exactly what comes next makes restarting easy. If you often feel cut off, try longer sessions or a count-up Flow timer for creative work.
