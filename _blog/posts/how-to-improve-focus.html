If it feels harder to concentrate than it used to, you're not imagining it. Phones, notifications, endless feeds and constant task-switching train our brains to expect something new every few seconds. Researcher Gloria Mark, who has studied attention at work for two decades, reports that people now switch their attention on screens far more often than they did in the early 2000s, typically within a minute.

The good news: focus is both a skill you can train and an environment you can design. Here's how to rebuild it, step by step.

First, understand what you're up against

  • Task-switching has a cost. Psychologist Sophie Leroy found that when you move from one task to another, part of your attention stays behind on the first. She called this attention residue. Quick checks of email or chat leave you working at partial capacity.
  • Getting back takes time. After an interruption, it can take a long time to return to where you were, which is why a day full of “quick” interruptions feels so unproductive.
  • Minds wander, a lot. A large 2010 study by Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert found people's minds were wandering in nearly half of the moments sampled. That's normal; the skill is noticing and coming back.

1. Do one thing at a time

Single-tasking is the foundation. Choose one task, close everything else and work only on that. It feels slow at first because your brain is used to the little reward of switching. Within a week or two, it starts to feel calm instead.

2. Work in timed focus sessions

“Focus all afternoon” is too big. “Focus for the next 25 minutes” is manageable. Timed sessions with planned breaks give your attention a clear job and a guaranteed rest. This is the heart of the Pomodoro Technique, and it's the most practical focus training there is.

3. Start where your focus is today

If 25 minutes feels impossible right now, start with 10 or 15. Finishing short sessions builds the habit and confidence. Then add five minutes every week or so. Trying to jump straight to 90-minute deep work sessions usually ends in frustration.

4. Remove interruptions before they happen

Willpower is a poor defence against a buzzing phone. Change the environment instead:

  • Turn on Do Not Disturb for focus sessions.
  • Put your phone out of sight, ideally in another room.
  • Close email and chat; check them at set times.
  • Tell people around you when you're in a focus block.

5. Catch your mind wandering, kindly

When you notice you've drifted, don't scold yourself. Just note it and return to the task. Some people make a tally mark each time. Noticing faster is the skill, and it improves with practice. Over weeks, you'll see the count fall.

6. Train attention directly

Mindfulness meditation is essentially attention practice: focus on your breath, notice when your mind wanders, come back. Studies have linked even short mindfulness training to better attention. In one 2013 study by Michael Mrazek and colleagues, two weeks of mindfulness training reduced mind-wandering and improved reading comprehension scores. Five to ten minutes a day is a reasonable start.

7. Protect your sleep

Sleep loss hits attention hard. If you're regularly sleeping badly, no focus technique will fully compensate. Consistent sleep and wake times, less screen time before bed and caffeine only earlier in the day all help.

8. Move your body

Regular exercise is linked to better attention and mood, and even a short walk can freshen you up for the next session. Use your breaks to move rather than scroll. Need ideas? See 25 Pomodoro break ideas.

9. Use sound to your advantage

Noisy surroundings drain attention. Steady background sounds like rain or brown noise can mask distractions. Avoid music with lyrics for reading and writing. More in choosing focus sounds.

10. Do your hardest work at your best time

Most people have a daily window when concentration comes more easily. Find yours and protect it for demanding work. Leave email and admin for the slump.

11. Track your focus to see progress

Improvement in focus is slow and easy to miss day to day. Tracking makes it visible: total focused time, session lengths, distractions per session. When you can see that you've gone from three distracted sessions a day to six calm ones, you'll keep going. Read how to track your focus time.
