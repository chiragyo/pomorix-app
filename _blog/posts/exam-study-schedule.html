Most exam study schedules die by day four. They're too ambitious, too rigid, or built around hours you don't actually have. The result is guilt, panic and a last-minute cram.

This guide shows you how to build a revision timetable that's realistic from the start, using focus sessions instead of vague hours, and how to keep it alive when life gets in the way.

Step 1: Count your real study time

Before writing a single topic, figure out how much time you actually have. Use focus sessions (for example 25-minute pomodoros) as your unit instead of hours. Sessions are honest: “3 hours in the library” might be four real sessions.

  1. Count the days until the exam.
  2. For each type of day, estimate realistic sessions. For example: class days = 4, weekends = 8, the final week = 10.
  3. Add them up, then subtract 15 to 20 percent as buffer for illness, surprises and bad days.

Example: 4 weeks with 5 class days (4 sessions) and 2 weekend days (8 sessions) per week gives (20 + 16) × 4 = 144 sessions. After a 20 percent buffer, you can plan about 115 sessions. That's roughly 48 hours of focused study.

Not sure what your realistic daily number is? Read how many pomodoros a day is realistic, or track a normal week first.

Step 2: List every topic and rate it

Go through your syllabus, past papers and notes and list every topic for every subject. Then rate each one:

  • Red: I don't understand this yet.
  • Amber: I understand it but can't answer questions reliably.
  • Green: I can answer exam questions on this.

Be honest. The point is to put your time where it makes the biggest difference.

Step 3: Give each topic a number of sessions

A simple rule of thumb:

RatingSessions to planWhat they're for
Red4–6Learn it, make notes or flashcards, practise, test
Amber2–3Practice questions and self-testing
Green1–2Quick review and a past-paper check

Add up the total. If it's more than the sessions you have, trim: combine small topics, cut green topics to one session, or focus on what's most likely to be examined. Also reserve about a quarter of your sessions for past papers, especially in the final two weeks.

Step 4: Spread topics out, don't block them

It's tempting to do “biology week”, then “chemistry week”. But memory research consistently favours spacing: revisiting a topic several times with gaps in between is far more effective than one long block.

A practical pattern for each topic:

  1. Learn: the first sessions where you understand and make notes or flashcards.
  2. Review after 1–2 days: a short self-test.
  3. Review after a week: practice questions.
  4. Final check: past-paper questions in the last two weeks.

So each day mixes two or three subjects: new learning in one, review in another, questions in a third.

Step 5: Build the weekly timetable

Here's an example class-day and weekend-day plan for three subjects:

DaySessionsPlan
Mon4Chem: learn equilibrium (2) · Bio: review cells (1) · Maths: 5 questions (1)
Tue4Maths: learn vectors (2) · Chem: review equilibrium (1) · Bio: flashcards (1)
Wed4Bio: learn genetics (2) · Maths: review vectors (1) · Chem: questions (1)
Thu4Chem: learn kinetics (2) · Bio: review genetics (1) · Maths: questions (1)
Fri3Light day: self-test on the week's topics (3)
Sat8Maths past paper (3) · Bio: new topic (3) · Chem: weekly review (2)
Sun4 + reviewCatch-up (buffer) · 15-minute weekly review and plan

Notice the Friday light day and Sunday buffer. Those are what keep the plan alive.

Step 6: Track what you actually do

A schedule tells you what you planned. Tracking tells you what happened. At the end of each day, record how many sessions you did and on what. Keep it effortless: a timer that logs sessions by subject does this for you.

Each week, compare plan and reality:

  • Which subject is getting less time than planned?
  • Which days did you consistently miss?
  • Are your red topics turning amber and green?

Step 7: Adjust every week

Every Sunday, spend 15 minutes on a review:

  1. Re-rate your topics (red, amber, green).
  2. Move unfinished sessions into next week or drop low-value ones.
  3. Adjust next week's daily sessions to match how much you really did.

A schedule that changes is not a failed schedule. It's a working one.

The final week before the exam

  • Shift to mostly past papers and self-testing, timed like the real exam.
  • Keep reviewing flashcards for your weakest topics daily.
  • Protect your sleep. It's when your brain consolidates what you learned.
  • Plan a lighter day before the exam: a short review, then rest.

Keep yourself going

Long revision periods are as much about motivation as planning. A daily goal and a streak make each day count, and seeing your total climb is genuinely encouraging.
