You pick up your phone to check one thing. Twenty minutes later you're watching a video about something you didn't care about an hour ago, and your study session has quietly ended. Sound familiar?

Phone checking is one of the biggest focus killers there is, and it's not about weak willpower. Phones and apps are designed to be hard to put down. Here's how the habit works and 10 practical ways to break it while you study or work.

Why it's so hard to stop

Phone checking follows a simple habit loop:

  1. Cue: a buzz, a moment of boredom, or (very often) the moment the work gets hard.
  2. Routine: unlock, open an app, scroll.
  3. Reward: something new: a message, a like, a funny clip.

Because the reward is unpredictable (sometimes there's something interesting, sometimes not), the habit gets even stronger. And notice that third cue: we often reach for the phone exactly when a task gets difficult. The phone isn't just a distraction; it's an escape hatch from discomfort.

Each check costs more than the minute it takes. Research on attention residue shows that switching tasks leaves part of your attention behind, and getting fully back into deep work takes time.

10 ways to stop checking your phone

1. Put it out of reach

The single most effective step. Another room is best. A bag across the room is good. Face down on your desk is barely better than nothing. If reaching the phone takes effort, most impulses pass before you act on them.

2. Turn on Do Not Disturb for focus time

Silence everything except true emergencies (you can allow calls from favourite contacts). If your phone can't buzz, one of the biggest cues disappears.

Pomorix can switch on Do Not Disturb by itself when a focus session starts and turn it off when the session ends. No more forgetting, and no more buzzes mid-session.

3. Work in timed sessions

“Don't check your phone all afternoon” is a vague promise that's easy to break. “Don't check it for 25 minutes; you can on the break” is concrete and easy to keep. The Pomodoro Technique gives your phone checking a time and a place.

4. Clean up your notifications

Go through your notification settings once. Keep calls, messages from real people and calendar reminders. Turn off everything else: social media, shopping, games, news, “suggestions”. You'll still see them when you choose to open the apps.

5. Move tempting apps off your home screen

Put social media and video apps in a folder on the last page, or log out after each use. Small bits of friction make automatic checking less automatic.

6. If your phone is your timer, make it boring

Using your phone as a study timer is convenient, but it's also how “just checking the time” becomes 15 minutes of scrolling. Solutions:

  • Use a focus app with no ads and no feed.
  • Keep the timer screen awake so you never need to unlock the phone to see the time.
  • Put the phone slightly out of reach, where you can see it but not grab it.
  • Or use a home screen widget that shows the timer without opening anything else.

7. Notice the urge, then wait 60 seconds

When you feel the pull, notice it (“there's the urge”) and wait a minute. Urges rise and fall like waves. Often the moment passes, especially if you return to the next small step of your task.

8. Count your slips

Every time you pick up your phone during a session, make a tally. You'll be surprised by the first day's number, and motivated as it falls. Measuring a habit changes it.

9. Plan phone time on breaks, with a limit

You don't have to give up your phone. Just give it a place: “I'll check messages on my long break.” Even then, be careful: endless feeds make 5-minute breaks last 20. A break timer that ends the break for you helps. Here are break ideas that don't involve your phone.

10. Replace the escape

If you reach for your phone when work gets hard, give yourself another escape: write the difficulty down, stand and stretch for 30 seconds, or break the task into a smaller step. You're replacing the routine while keeping the cue.
